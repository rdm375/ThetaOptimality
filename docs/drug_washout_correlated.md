# Correlated assay errors in drug washout

## Question this experiment answers

The preceding washout example separated measurement uncertainty from time
discretization error while treating assay errors as independent. This follow-on
holds the same physical model and observation times, but lets successive plasma
assays share correlated error. It asks how that covariance changes the fit,
initial-concentration uncertainty, and the times at which concentration and
remaining exposure can be qualified.

The physical model remains deliberately simple and has an exact solution:

$$
\dot c(t)=-k c(t),\qquad c(t_0)=c_0,\qquad
k=0.45\;\mathrm{h}^{-1},\quad t_0=0,\quad t_f=8\;\mathrm h.
$$

Thus $c(t;c_0)=c_0e^{-kt}$ and

$$
A(t;c_0)=\int_t^{t_f}c(s;c_0)\,ds
=\frac{c_0}{k}\left(e^{-kt}-e^{-kt_f}\right).
$$

The unknown is the initial plasma concentration $c_0$ in mg/L. The elimination
rate is known and there is no absorption, multicompartment distribution, or
parameter uncertainty. This is a numerical-method benchmark, not a drug-dosing
model.

## Why observations can be correlated

Several assays can share a batch calibration error. A drifting instrument can
also make consecutively processed samples more alike than independent draws.
The benchmark uses the latter as a simple serial-error model. Six plasma
concentrations are observed at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h. Their errors have
marginal standard deviation $\sigma=0.020$ mg/L and AR(1) correlation
$\rho=0.70$ by sample order:

$$
R_{ij}=\sigma^2\rho^{|i-j|}.
$$

The dataset is fixed for both covariance assumptions. It is generated by
filtering the fixed standardized innovations

$$
z=(0.7,-0.4,0.6,-0.2,-0.5,0.3)^T
$$

through the AR(1) model, then adding the resulting error vector to the exact
state for $c_0=10$ mg/L. Its standardized squared norm is $z^Tz=1.39$, below
$\chi^2_{6,0.95}=12.59158724$. Consequently the true initial concentration is
inside the nominal 95% joint residual ellipsoid for this generated realization.

## Jointly weighted discrete objective

Let $y_i$ be the fixed observations and $s_i=e^{-kt_i}$. The exact continuous
reference is the generalized least-squares fit

$$
\widehat c_0=\frac{\mathbf s^T\mathbf R^{-1}\mathbf y}
{\mathbf s^T\mathbf R^{-1}\mathbf s}.
$$

For each theta grid, the library instead fits the discrete sample sensitivities
$s_{h,i}$ produced by its own forward solve:

$$
J_h(c_0)=\frac12\mathbf r_h(c_0)^T\mathbf R^{-1}\mathbf r_h(c_0),
\qquad \mathbf r_h(c_0)=c_0\mathbf s_h-\mathbf y.
$$

The example runs this with both the correct AR(1) covariance and a deliberately
misspecified diagonal covariance with the same marginal standard deviations.
The second fit is a comparison only. Since the data were generated with AR(1)
errors, the diagonal model's nominal 95% interval is not a calibrated 95%
confidence statement for this data-generating process.

For the AR(1) case, the precision matrix $\mathbf W=\mathbf R^{-1}$ is symmetric
positive definite and tridiagonal. With $m=6$ it has endpoint diagonal entries
$1/[\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)]$, interior diagonal entries
$(1+\rho^2)/[\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)]$, and adjacent off-diagonal entries
$-\rho/[\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)]$. The discrete objective is evaluated as one
cross-time term, not as six independent residual penalties.

## What changed in the shared C++20 library

The former objective API handled independent observations as a sum of nodal
scalar costs. It could not represent the off-diagonal terms in
$\mathbf r^T\mathbf R^{-1}\mathbf r$. The new `CorrelatedObservationTerm`
stores observation nodes, residual callbacks, and a precision matrix. The
library now:

1. evaluates the joint quadratic objective;
2. forms all components of $\mathbf W\mathbf r$ before inserting their state
   and direct-control derivatives at the corresponding nodes;
3. sends those combined sources through the ordinary reverse discrete adjoint;
4. differentiates the source in the incremental adjoint, including both the
   $\mathbf J^T\mathbf W\mathbf J$ term and residual-curvature terms;
5. rejects malformed, nonsymmetric, or non-positive-definite precision matrices.

The validation suite compares the correlated gradient and Hessian-vector
product against finite differences of the same objective and checks invalid
precision matrices. The washout application additionally compares the
theta-discrete fit, gradient, Hessian, and exposure adjoint against analytical
references.

## Joint uncertainty and qualification

The correlated measurement-consistent set is defined by

$$
\left\{c_0\in[0,20]:
\mathbf r(c_0)^T\mathbf R^{-1}\mathbf r(c_0)
\le \chi^2_{6,0.95}\right\}.
$$

Because the observation model is linear in $c_0$, the boundary equation is a
scalar quadratic and gives an exact interval. The same construction is applied
to the diagonal comparator using its assumed covariance. **The independent
Gaussian noise-box formula is not used.** The correct AR(1) confidence interval
has nominal 95% coverage under the stated known-covariance Gaussian model; the
diagonal interval is included to show the effect of incorrectly ignoring
correlation.

The goal tolerances are 0.020 mg/L for concentration and 0.060 mg h/L for
remaining AUC. The C++ program separates the exact covariance-induced spread
from the theta discretization discrepancy. For the latter, it compares the
piecewise-linear reconstruction of the theta nodes with the analytic trajectory
of the continuous weighted fit and checks the maximum discrepancy on each full
step. A goal qualifies only if the sum of these two terms stays below tolerance
for all later intervals through 8 h.

## Results and interpretation

For this fixed realization, the analytical fits are 10.01576 mg/L with AR(1)
errors and 10.01537 mg/L under the diagonal assumption. The fitted value moves
by only 0.000384 mg/L. The larger effect is uncertainty: the 95% interval width
is 0.16466 mg/L for the correlated model and 0.12619 mg/L for the diagonal
assumption, about 30% wider when correlation is retained.

On the refined $N=512$, $\theta=0.5$ grid:

| Model | Concentration qualifies from | Remaining AUC qualifies from |
|---|---:|---:|
| Correct AR(1) covariance | 3.15625 h | 2.31250 h |
| Diagonal covariance assumption | 2.56250 h | 1.76562 h |

Thus this example's main lesson is not that correlated weighting must greatly
move the best fit. Here the fitted value barely changes, while ignoring
correlation materially narrows the uncertainty set and makes both goals appear
qualified earlier. On the coarse $N=32$ grid, the numerical discrepancy is
already below these tolerances; the qualification-time difference is driven by
the measurement covariance rather than by time-step accuracy.

The convergence study verifies seven quantities for both covariance choices:
state, tangent, cost, gradient, Hessian, recovered initial concentration, and
theta-quadrature AUC. Midpoint is second order; $\theta=0.65$ is first order.
This separates correctness of the discrete optimality system from the physical
conclusion drawn from the measurement uncertainty.

## Reproduce

```bash
make correlated-washout
make correlated-washout-plots
```

The C++ executable writes fits, covariance intervals, qualification decisions,
error budgets, convergence rates, and checks to
`results/drug_washout_correlated/`. Python only renders these C++ outputs; it
does no fitting, statistical inference, or qualification.
